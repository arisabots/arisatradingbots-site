Why do our bots win small and lose big? We tested whether they trade the wrong side - they don't, and we changed three stop-losses

Demo bots, no real money involved. Three stop distances widened on test evidence that is not yet confirmed live; each has a loss limit that reverts it

Across seven of our eight rule-based demo bots the average winning trade is about a third the size of the average loser, so they need to win 66-76% of the time just to break even. Only the Dow and S&P bots clear that bar, and they are the only two profitable bots. The obvious suspicion was that the bots are on the wrong side of the market. We compared every real entry with an entry at a random moment in the same hours: the entries are mostly coin flips (a few short-term positives for the Russell, soybean and Dow bots, two negatives for oil and sugar), not systematically wrong. The shape of the losses is the exit design: a stop closes the whole position while profits are banked in small pieces.

We then tested the two obvious ways to change that. Removing the three staged profit-takes made the fleet worse in 22 of 24 bot-and-window comparisons (full-period total from +$427 to +$288). Loosening the trailing profit lock, or removing the trailing stop, was worse still (+$520 to between +$207 and +$379). Both stay as they are. The one lever that held up was stop width, and it came with a finding about our own tools: our test tool judged a stop on the closing price, so a spike through the stop that recovered by the close never stopped the test out, which made tight stops look free. We added a mode that judges stops the way the broker does.

With that, three stops were widened: Russell 1.5% to 1.2% (a clear plateau), Nasdaq 0.35% to 1.5% (it was being hit in 61% of its trades; the evidence is suggestive, not a plateau) and silver 0.6% to 1.5% (every width from 1.5% to 3% was positive in all three windows). A losing stop now costs more per trade, so each change has a limit that reverts it: Russell -$60, Nasdaq -$50, silver -$25. We left the other bots alone: sugar's test favoured a much wider stop but is biased by a setting fitted to the old broken stops, and the rest were noise.

We got things wrong along the way and fixed them. Our first run said removing the profit-takes was better for the soybean bot; that was our test tool letting two simultaneous runs overwrite each other's state, and the corrected number reverses it. A "no trailing stop" setting also had a typo and silently did nothing until a duplicate result gave it away.

2026-10-26 (evening)

Our sugar bot's prices were rounded to the nearest 0.001 - about half a percent - so its signals and its stop-losses were off

Demo bot, no real money involved. A real bug, fixed and restarted; the strategy itself is still about break-even in testing

After the sugar bot's first loss under the 10-22 changes, its logs showed the momentum indicator jumping from 40 to 15 to 61 within ten minutes on tiny price moves, and our test tool refused to produce the trade the live bot had taken. The cause: the price feed rounded every sugar price to three decimals, a leftover from the index bots whose prices are in the thousands. Sugar trades near 0.18, so each step was about half a percent. The signals were computed on a staircase, and the broker stop was placed from the rounded price, so stops that should have been 0.8% away were between 0.83% and 1.08% away (median 0.99%) across 21 trades. That also explains the "stops fill at 1% against an 0.8% stop" puzzle we had put down to slippage. And our tests had validated a signal the live bot was not trading.

The same replay with the rounding switched on went from about +$12 to -$734 over 13 months. We now keep five decimals (what the broker quotes) and added tests. The first session after the restart: big indicator jumps fell from 7.5% of readings to 0.4%, the stop landed exactly 0.80% from the signal price, and the one trade, a buy at 13:35, closed at the profit lock for +$4.78. One trade proves nothing; even at full precision the sugar strategy only breaks even in testing, so this fixes a mismatch but does not create an edge.

A price-feed hiccup turned into a fake price that closed a real position - eight bots had the same flaw

Demo bots, no real money involved. A real bug found and fixed in all nine demo bots; the cause of the feed hiccups themselves is still unknown, but we now capture what OANDA says when they happen

Two losses on our soybean bot about a minute apart in the early hours (about $11 and $22) turned out not to be stop-outs. The bot's price feed had briefly failed: our broker's price service answered "401 Unauthorized" to both the live price and the backup request. When that happens our price fetcher quietly falls back to a made-up placeholder price. For the soybean bot that placeholder was 51,700 - a leftover number copied from our Dow bot - when soybeans trade near 12.4. The bot read that as a gain of over 400,000% on its open trade, fired its profit-taking rule and sold part of the position at the real market price (a loss), then its trailing stop, armed on the fake high, sold the rest on the next cycle.

The log shows the same signature on soybean losses on 13 October and, very likely, 14 October, and error counts show most of our other demo bots have hit the same failure at some point. Our launch code never checked the "this is a placeholder" flag the fetcher already returned. Some bots' placeholders were closer to real prices (the S&P 500 bot's was only about 4% off), which would have made it harder to notice.

Fixed in all nine demo bots (including the gold bot, which has the same fallback): when the price is a placeholder and the bot is meant to be using the broker's feed, it now skips that cycle entirely - no trading decisions and no software stop or profit-take - and warns us at most every 15 minutes. The stop-loss orders resting at the broker stay in force throughout. We also made the fetcher retry a 401 twice with a short pause, and record every failed response (status, error text and the broker's request ID, never the password key) to a log file. All nine bots were restarted with this tonight.

What we don't know: why the 401s happen. They are rare and patchy: two bots failed in the same minute while six others were fine, and the same key worked seconds later. A two-minute test of 960 requests produced no errors, so it isn't simply too many requests. Our nine demo bots all share one key, which is one thing we can change. The new log should give us a request ID we can hand to the broker the next time it happens.

2026-10-19 (evening)

Our own test runs had been writing into the live trading bots' logs

Demo bots, no real money involved. A bookkeeping flaw in our testing tools, found and fixed across all nine demo bots; no trade was affected

While reading the gold bot's log to work out why it was idle, we noticed it was full of lines that the live bot never wrote: thousands of simulated trades and model readouts. They came from us. Each of our bots writes a copy of its screen output into a log file as soon as its code is loaded, and our offline replay tool loads the same code. So every test we ran, by hand, in the weekly model check, and in the automatic check that watches each bot's market direction, quietly appended its simulation output to the running bot's log.

It did not change any trading, but it made the logs misleading and much larger than they should be, and it risked us reading simulated lines as live behaviour. We re-checked the conclusions we had drawn from that log by counting only the bot's own live evaluations, and they held (for example, the gold bot's signals really are about 85% sell).

Fixed in all nine demo bots: the replay tool now switches that log capture off before it loads the bot's code. We confirmed that running a replay no longer grows the live log, and added a test per bot so it can't quietly come back. This only touches the test tools, so no bot needed a restart. The old simulated lines are still in the logs; we've left them rather than edit history.

Our real-money forex bot keeps losing a little on every trade - the broker's own records showed where the money goes

Real money, small stakes: the account is under £50 after losing about £100 since March. A protection against the daily rollover and a pair restriction went live this evening; the effect isn't measured yet

Our forex bot wins about 43% of its trades, and the average win is about the same size as the average loss, so it quietly loses a few pence every trade. We pulled every fill from the broker's own records (1,719 closed trades) rather than trusting our simulator, which only holds old data for a few pairs and approximates how trades exit. Since July the bot is roughly break-even on price movement alone (+£2 over 928 trades) and loses £25 once the spread is paid. The signals are close to a coin flip, and the spread eats the rest.

One leak stood out, and it is mechanical rather than a statistical guess. Every day at 5pm New York time the forex market rolls over and spreads blow up for a few minutes: about £0.13 per fill that hour, against about £0.017 normally. Positions held through it get stopped out by the spike. 52 of the bot's stop-losses filled at the same second, 21:04:55 UTC, on different days. That hour lost £39 across the record, in both the first and second half of it. We also found that the bot's "don't trade at rollover" setting had never worked: it lived in a file the live bot doesn't use, and it only ever blocked new entries, not open positions.

What we changed: the bot now closes open positions in the five minutes before the rollover and doesn't open new ones until it has passed. The times follow New York time, so the US clock change on 1 November moves them an hour on its own. We also stopped it trading three pairs (GBP/CAD, GBP/JPY, CAD/JPY) that lost money in both halves of the record. Applied to the past record, both changes together would have turned the second half from a £18 loss into about break-even, though the pair choice is partly hindsight and the real effect could be smaller.

What we don't know: we could not test trading less often or being pickier on entries, because the strength of each signal isn't recorded with each fill. The bot restarted on 19 October with both changes. First result, from the broker's records: at 20:55 UTC it closed all seven positions it had open, within about a second, at a combined price result of about -£0.01 plus roughly £0.09 of spread, and it opened nothing during the rollover. The first new entry came at 21:10, as soon as the block ended. There were no rollover stop-outs that night, and the one blocked pair that still had an open position was closed rather than left running. One night proves little, though: over the record a stop-out hit in that hour on about four days in ten, so a quiet night is the likely outcome even without the change. We will revisit after about two weeks of fills. Even at best, this looks like moving from losing slowly to roughly break-even, not to making money.

Looking into one bot's bad week turned up stop-outs that never made it into our own trade log - across the whole demo fleet

Demo bots, no real money involved. Four losing trades missing from our records over seven weeks; nothing about the trading itself was wrong, only the bookkeeping

While checking why our soybean bot lost money this week, the broker's own transaction history showed a stop-loss fill that our trade log had no record of. We compared the broker's closing fills against each demo bot's log since late August. Four stop-outs were missing in total, on four different bots (about $9 to $50 each). The other bots matched exactly.

The cause is in how a bot keeps track of its own position. Each bot periodically re-checks the broker and, if the broker says it is flat, forgets its local position. That step forgot the position without recording how it ended, so whichever code path noticed first decided whether the loss was written down. The same code was copied into all nine bots.

Fixing it exposed a second, related flaw: if the broker could not be reached at that moment, the same step treated the failed check as "flat" and dropped a position the broker still held. Left alone, a fix that records closes would have recorded closes that never happened, so the check now leaves the position alone when it cannot confirm anything.

Each bot now records a broker-side close before it re-checks, tested with simulated stop-outs and failed connections. The fix is live on all nine demo bots (restarted the same day), and we added the four missing trades back to the history, each marked as backfilled and taken from the broker's own transaction record. After that, every demo bot's logged closes match the broker's fill-for-fill. Those four trades were all losses, so our published totals for those bots were slightly better than the broker's own numbers until the backfill.
